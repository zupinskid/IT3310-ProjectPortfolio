## 1. Projects versus operations

A project is temporary work with a defined objective and an endpoint, while an operation is ongoing work that keeps an existing service running. For example, replacing a clinic's patient intake and scheduling system is an IT project because the team must configure the new system, migrate data, test it, and complete an approved rollout. Providing daily helpdesk support for that system after launch is an IT operation because staff repeatedly resolve access issues and support users. The transition occurs when the new system is accepted and responsibility for routine support moves to the operations team.

## 2. Delivery approach

I would recommend a predictive approach as the strongest starting point because the hospital must complete substantial documentation and obtain regulatory approval before implementing the replacement patient records system. Defined requirements, documented testing, approval milestones, and formal change control would help the team demonstrate readiness and understand how changes affect scope, cost, and schedule. The team could still use short feedback cycles to refine workflows during planning and testing, but implementation should remain dependent on the required approvals.

## 3. Role clarity

The Product Manager defines the product's direction and value by understanding user needs and deciding which outcomes and capabilities deserve priority. The Project Manager coordinates delivery by managing the schedule, budget, risks, dependencies, and stakeholder communication. Their work connects when the Product Manager identifies a valuable capability and the Project Manager helps assess the time, resources, and delivery tradeoffs needed to provide it. For RCHN, they would work together to prioritize a scheduling improvement and agree on a feasible release plan. Confusion could cause problems if the Product Manager promises a release date without checking capacity, or if the Project Manager drops an important patient workflow simply to protect the schedule without consulting the Product Manager and affected stakeholders. Clear decision responsibilities and coordinated change discussions would help prevent conflicting commitments and rework.

## Initial RCHN engagement brief

### Why this is a project

Replacing RCHN's aging intake and scheduling system will create a new operating capability through a temporary, coordinated effort. The replacement should have an authorized scope, agreed success criteria, and a defined handoff to the teams that will support it. Running the existing system and supporting clinic staff remain ongoing operations during that effort.

### What NovaTech needs to learn

- Which intake and scheduling problems most affect patients and staff, and what evidence shows their impact.
- How workflows differ across the six clinics and who should provide requirements and approve decisions.
- Which data, integrations, privacy requirements, accessibility needs, and operational constraints the replacement must address.
- What funding, staff availability, timeline constraints, and measures of success the sponsor will authorize.

### Initial approach

A blended approach is a reasonable working hypothesis for the broader RCHN engagement: establish formal authorization, compliance and readiness checkpoints, and a controlled rollout, while using demonstrations and staff feedback to refine uncertain workflows. This is a preliminary recommendation, not an approved delivery decision; NovaTech should validate it after learning more about the requirements and constraints. The next portfolio artifact will begin formalizing the engagement through a project charter draft.
